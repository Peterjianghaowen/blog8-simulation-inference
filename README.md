# How Much Evidence Does a Survey Buy? Bootstrap Intervals, Coverage and Hypothesis Tests

Analysis code for Blog Post 8 of AEDS 6400 (Computational Methods for
Economists): a simulation-based explanation of statistical inference, built on a
real question about the U.S. unemployment rate.

**Write-up (with the interactive figure embedded):**
<https://Peterjianghaowen.github.io/personalwebsite/blog/posts/post8/index.html>

## Why this question

Every month the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes an unemployment rate, and
every month the news reports how it moved. But a number that is reported is not
the same as a number that is known. The CPS that produces the rate is a sample
of about 75,000-100,000 people, and the rate it produces carries sampling
error: if the same people were not the same people, the rate would be
different.

This repository answers three questions that the monthly rate raises but never
answers:

1. **How precise is the rate?** A bootstrap confidence interval, built by
   resampling the people who were interviewed.
2. **Does that interval really mean what it says?** A 95% interval is supposed
   to contain the truth about 95% of the time. We check by simulation, and we
   find that on the real CPS data the textbook interval contains the truth only
   about 80% of the time.
3. **How much evidence would a different survey have produced?** The same
   comparison - January 2019 against January 2024 - is decisive with the full
   CPS sample and meaningless with a sample of a few thousand people. The
   verdict is a property of the data, not of the world.

## Data

All data come from the **IPUMS Current Population Survey (CPS) basic monthly
microdata**, January 2019 - August 2026. Two months are used in the analysis:
**January 2019** (the pre-pandemic reference month) and **January 2024** (the
month under study). Comparing the same month in different years removes the
seasonal pattern of the labour market from the comparison.

The raw extract is **not redistributed here**: IPUMS asks that extracts not be
shared, and the file is large. `code/01_fetch_cps.R` recreates it exactly.

### Sample rules

* Civilian noninstitutional population aged 16 or over (`AGE >= 16`,
  `EMPSTAT != 1`).
* In the labour force: `LABFORCE == 2`; unemployed: `EMPSTAT` in
  {20, 21, 22}; employed: `EMPSTAT` in {10, 12}.
* Every population statistic uses the CPS final person weight (`WTFINL`), and
  the unemployment rate is unemployed divided by the labour force, the BLS
  definition.

The rates computed here are **not seasonally adjusted**, so they differ from
the seasonally adjusted headline numbers published by the BLS. Because both
months compared are January, the seasonal pattern cancels out of the
comparison.

## How to reproduce

From the repository root:

```bash
Rscript code/01_fetch_cps.R   # download the IPUMS extract (needs an API key)
Rscript code/02_clean.R        # build the person-level analysis file
Rscript code/03_bootstrap.R    # bootstrap intervals, p-value, the smaller-survey experiment
Rscript code/04_simulate.R     # coverage, the null distribution, power
Rscript code/05_figures.R      # the figures
```

or simply:

```bash
./run_all.sh
```

Required R packages: `ipumsr`, `dplyr`, `readr`, `ggplot2`, `plotly`,
`htmlwidgets`, `cowplot`. Script 05 needs `pandoc` to save the widget as a
self-contained HTML file; on macOS, RStudio ships a copy, and the script points
`RSTUDIO_PANDOC` at it when `pandoc` is not on the PATH.

The whole pipeline takes roughly five minutes, most of it in
`code/03_bootstrap.R` (1,000 simulated surveys at six sample sizes).

## Pipeline

| Script | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `01_fetch_cps.R` | Downloads the monthly CPS extract through the IPUMS API (only the six variables the analysis needs). |
| `02_clean.R` | Applies the sample rules, builds the person-level file for the two focal months and the national monthly series in `results/monthly_rates.csv`. |
| `03_bootstrap.R` | Bootstraps the two rates and the change between them, turns the bootstrap distribution into a null distribution for a simulation-based p-value, and runs the "what if we had interviewed fewer people?" experiment. |
| `04_simulate.R` | Runs the coverage simulations (by sample size and by confidence level), simulates the null distribution and the power curves, and writes the data behind the interactive figure. |
| `05_figures.R` | Draws the four static figures and saves the interactive widget. |

## Results

Every number below is produced by the code and stored in `results/`.

### The estimate and its interval

| | January 2019 | January 2024 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People interviewed | 97,181 | 80,923 |
| Unemployment rate | 4.47% | 4.12% |
| Bootstrap 95% interval | 4.29% - 4.67% | 3.92% - 4.32% |

The estimated change is **-0.35 percentage points**, with a bootstrap 95%
interval of **-0.64 to -0.08** points. The textbook (Wald) interval for the
same change is **-0.54 to -0.16** points.

The bootstrap interval is **49% wider**. That is not a defect of the
bootstrap: it is the design effect of the CPS. The textbook standard error
treats the sample as a simple random sample of equal-weight people, but the
CPS weights range from 177 to 13,140 with a coefficient of variation of 0.54,
so the true sampling error is much larger than the formula says. The
simulation-based p-value is **0.016** rather than the textbook **0.0002**.

### Does a 95% interval contain the truth 95% of the time?

Simulating a population with a known rate and sampling it over and over
(`results/coverage.csv`):

| Nominal level | n = 250 | n = 1,000 | n = 80,923 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 80% | 82.7% | 82.7% | 79.6% |
| 90% | 90.6% | 92.0% | 89.6% |
| 95% | 93.2% | 94.9% | 95.0% |
| 99% | 97.3% | 99.3% | 99.4% |

The interval does what it promises, and the promise changes with the
confidence level, exactly as advertised.

On the **real** CPS data the picture is less comfortable. Repeating the
whole experiment with 1,000 simulated surveys of n people per month
(`results/small_sample.csv`), the bootstrap interval contains the full-sample
estimate about **92%** of the time and the textbook interval only about
**80%** of the time. The bootstrap is honest about the skewness of a weighted
proportion; the textbook interval is not honest at all.

### The null distribution and the p-value

Under H0 ("nothing changed"), the estimated change has a sampling
distribution centred on zero. Recentring the bootstrap draws on zero gives
that distribution, and the share of it beyond what we observed is the
p-value: **0.016**. The rejection region is the 2.5% in each tail, beyond
+/- 0.28 percentage points, and the observed change of -0.35 points sits just
inside it.

The simulated null distribution is standard normal to within
**D = 0.018 (KS p = 0.50)**; an independent simulation of the experiment from
scratch gives 10th and 90th percentiles of -1.25 and +1.33 against the
normal's -1.28 and +1.28.

### Power: how much could this survey have found?

The power to detect the change we actually measured, -0.35 points:

| People per month | 1,000 | 10,000 | 25,000 | 80,923 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Power | 5.8% | 13.4% | 26.5% | 66.8% |

A survey of 1,000 people per month would have missed this real change 94% of
the time. Even the full CPS sample has a one-in-three chance of missing it.

## Limitations

* The CPS is a complex probability sample (geographic stratification,
  household clusters, unequal weights). The simulations here treat it as a
  simple random sample of people, so the true sampling error of the published
  rate is at least as large as the bootstrap suggests.
* The bootstrap interval is slightly conservative for the weighted proportion:
  it covers about 92% of the time rather than 95%, because the weighted rate
  is a skewed statistic and the percentile method is not centred perfectly on
  it.
* January 2024 is five years after January 2019, so the comparison mixes the
  pandemic and its recovery. Nothing here identifies a cause; the question is
  only how much of the measured difference is sampling noise.
* The "smaller survey" experiment resamples the people we have, so it
  inherits the same design, including the unequal weights.
